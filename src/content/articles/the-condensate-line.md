This is the other summer water. Not the swamp cooler.

A refrigerated air conditioner takes moisture out of the house and has to put it somewhere. That somewhere is a drain pan and a line, usually a short run of PVC that terminates at the side of the house. If that line is aimed at the dirt next to the stem wall — and a lot of them are — it drips in the same six square feet from the first hot week until someone shuts the unit off in the fall.

It does not look like a problem. It looks like a pipe doing what a pipe does. The soil next to the foundation does not care what it looks like. Expansive clay will take that drip all summer and swell. A door that was fine in April starts to bind in July. A crack at a window corner opens a little. By October the door swings again and the crack is narrower. Settlement from a bearing problem does not do that. A wet-dry cycle against one wall does.

Collapsible soil takes the same water differently. It does not need repeated wetting and drying — it needs to get wet enough, for long enough, one time. A condensate line dripping in the same spot all summer can be exactly that. Unlike the clay, that movement does not walk back in the fall. Once the soil structure collapses, it stays collapsed.

The line is easy to miss because it is small and because it is working as designed. Nobody installed it to wreck the clay. They installed it to get water away from the unit. Away from the unit and onto the foundation are not the same destination.

Check the termination. If it is dripping into a hole it has worn in the dirt, that is the first move — pipe it to daylight, to a splash block that actually drains, to anywhere that is not the soil against the house. Monsoon and irrigation can be feeding the same wall in the same months. The condensate line is just the one you can find in thirty seconds with a hose in your hand.

If the door still binds after the water is gone, that is a different question. It is not the first question.
