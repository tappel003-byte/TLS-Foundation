The original part of this house sat on native ground. The addition, put on later, sat on fill brought in to level a slope the first builder had worked around. On the surface it reads as one slab, one house, one problem. Underneath it was never one soil profile.

Fill does not behave like the ground it sits on. How differently depends on how it was placed and what is under it. Fill compacted onto solid native material can perform reasonably well. Fill sitting on soft, compressible native soil is another situation — the weight of the fill, plus the structure on top of it, keeps loading a layer that was never particularly stable. It can keep settling long after everyone has stopped thinking about the addition.

That was this house. The addition end was sinking, slowly. The original section wasn't, or at least not at the same rate, in the same direction. The crack at the seam where old met new wasn't cosmetic. It was the two sides of the house disagreeing about how much they had moved.

A repair that only treats the crack treats it as one problem with one cause. It isn't. It is two profiles, attached to each other, doing two different things. Piers under the addition have to reach past the fill and into competent native material below it. That depth is set by the fill, not by the floor plan. Piers under the original section — native soil that was never the problem — may not be warranted, or they need a different depth and spacing if they are.

Not every New Mexico house is built this way. Sometimes the original is the fill and the addition is the better ground. Sometimes both ends are fill. The point is the same: find the boundary before you treat the slab as one soil.

Treating both ends identically because they share a slab is the shortcut that produces a repair that half-works. Half a fix on a problem like this isn't fifty percent progress. It's a house that is still going to move, just more slowly, until someone finds the two soils that were there the entire time.
