title: "Fill Under the Slab, Native Soil Under the Addition"

summary: "A house with an original section and a later addition sat on two different soil profiles just a few feet apart -- one on native ground, one on decades-old fill. The two ends were moving for different reasons and needed different answers, not one repair applied evenly across both."
publishDate: 2026-09-10
---

The original part of the house was built on native ground. The addition, put on sometime later, sat on fill brought in to level out a slope that the original builder had simply worked around. On the surface, it reads as one slab, one house, one problem. Underneath, it was never one soil profile.

Fill behaves differently than the ground it's sitting on, and how differently depends entirely on how it was placed and what it's sitting on top of. Fill that was properly compacted onto solid native material can perform reasonably well. Fill sitting on top of soft, compressible native soil is a different situation — the weight of the fill itself, plus the structure on top of it, keeps loading a layer that was never particularly stable to begin with, and it keeps settling long after everyone involved has stopped thinking about it.

That was the situation here. The addition's end of the house was sinking, slowly, and the original section wasn't — or at least wasn't at the same rate, in the same direction. A crack running through the seam where old met new wasn't cosmetic. It was the two sides of the house literally disagreeing about how much they'd moved.

The complication is that a repair addressing only the symptom — the crack at the seam — treats it as one problem with one cause, when it's actually two profiles with two different behaviors that happen to be attached to each other. Piers under the addition need to reach past the fill and into competent native material below it, which in a case like this can mean going considerably deeper than anyone would guess just from looking at the ground floor plan. Piers under the original section, sitting on native soil that was never the problem, may not be warranted at all — or need a completely different depth and spacing to do anything useful.

Treating both ends identically because they're part of the same slab is exactly the kind of shortcut that produces a repair that half-works. Half a fix on a problem like this isn't fifty percent progress. It's a house that's still going to move, just more slowly, until someone goes back and finds the boundary between the two soils that was there the entire time.
