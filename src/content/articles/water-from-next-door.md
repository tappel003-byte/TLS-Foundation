The wet side of the house was not the side with the cooler or the downspout. It was the side that faces the neighbor.

In parts of New Mexico that is an acequia, a ditch, a low spot that has carried irrigation for a hundred years. In a subdivision it is a lawn that gets watered every morning, a new pad of concrete that changed where the runoff goes, a grade that was fine until someone built a wall. The water does not care whose meter it came from. The clay on your lot takes it anyway.

One wall sticks. The opposite wall does not. Cracks open on the wet elevation and stay quiet on the dry one. A bid that piers the whole perimeter is answering a different house than the one that is actually moving.

The first look is over the fence and at the dirt. Where does their water leave their lot. Where does yours sit. Sometimes the fix is a conversation and a splash block. Sometimes it is regrading your own side so their overflow has someplace to go that is not your stem wall. Sometimes the ditch is older than both houses and you are not going to move it — you are going to keep water from ponding against the foundation and then decide if the clay has already done enough damage to warrant more than that.

Piers do not turn off a neighbor's hose. If the moisture source is still running, the seasonal cycle is still running. Find that before you treat the wet wall like a bearing failure.
