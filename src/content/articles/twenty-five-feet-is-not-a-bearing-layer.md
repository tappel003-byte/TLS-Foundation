title: "Twenty-Five Feet Is Not a Bearing Layer"
description: "A proposed pier depth that sounds deep enough can still land ten feet below the water table -- and depth alone doesn't mean competent ground."
summary: "A bid specified a pier depth of twenty-five feet without ever mentioning where the water table actually sat -- which turned out to be roughly ten feet above the planned tip. A number that sounds deep enough is still a guess until someone confirms what's actually at that elevation; a bearing layer is a depth with a reason behind it, not a package depth that sounded finished."
publishDate: 2027-10-11
---

The number came from the person running the job. Twenty-five feet. That would do it.

It would also have put the pier about ten feet below the water table.

A shaft does not "stop" in that zone just because the proposal ran out of tape. Soft, saturated ground doesn't give you a place to park a helix and call it capacity. The plates keep turning, or they sit in material that won't hold a house because it is water and fines, not a layer. Depth below the water table is not automatically deeper-and-therefore-better. It can be deeper-and-still-in-the-problem.

The water table is an elevation. It is sitting there whether the bid mentions it or not. If the planned tip is ten feet under that line, you are not aiming at competent ground. You are aiming at a number that sounded finished.

Ask what they are going to. If the answer is twenty-five feet and nobody has said where the water is, or what the soil is doing at that elevation, you were handed a package depth. Package depths do not hold houses. Bearing layers do. Sometimes that layer is above the water. Sometimes you change tools because there isn't a layer where the number wanted one.

The house does not care about twenty-five. It cares whether the load ever left the dirt that was failing — and whether that dirt is even dirt at the depth they sold.
