The shaft was turning. Then it wasn't turning the way it had been.

Caliche is a hard, cemented layer in a lot of New Mexico ground. A helical pier is a steel shaft with plates, rotated in until the plates are in soil that can carry the load. Torque is the field read on whether that soil is there. When the plates hit caliche, torque climbs fast and depth stops making sense relative to the plan. That is not a mystery. That is the ground telling you the layer you just met is not the layer you were threading through.

The wrong move is to treat the spike as success and call it bearing. High torque in a thin hard cap is not the same thing as capacity at depth. The right move is to stop and decide what the layer actually is — a crust you can get through, a cap you should predrill, or a condition that wants a different tool.

Sometimes the helix goes through and finds what it was supposed to find underneath. Sometimes it doesn't. Micropiles exist for a reason: hard rock, cobbles, dense caliche, depth that a helix cannot honestly claim. Forcing the same shaft because "this is the pier we brought" is how you get a short pile with a good-looking torque number and a house that is still sitting on the problem.

The number on the gauge is information. It is not a verdict by itself. Depth, the soil above and below the hard layer, and whether the plates actually opened into something that can hold a house — those are the rest of the sentence. Caliche just makes you write that sentence on the spot instead of at the office later.
