A floor-level survey is a map. Elevations on a plan. A pattern. Areas that are high, areas that are low, and whether that pattern looks like a house moving or a house that was built that way.

It is not a home inspection. It will not tell you about the roof or the furnace.

It is not a geotechnical investigation. Nobody drills a boring and nobody writes a soil report from a walk-across with a level.

It is not a stamped recommendation. A document that tells you what to do to the foundation is engineering. That is a different piece of work, from a licensed engineer, and it costs more and takes longer because it is allowed to tell you what to do.

It is not a repair bid. The visit is for the map. If someone leaves a pier proposal on the same clipboard as the elevations, they ran two jobs at once. Those jobs should not be the same visit.

What it is for: seeing whether the floors agree with the cracks and the doors, or whether they don't. A slab that is high at a wet corner and low somewhere else is a different story than a slab that is out of level the same way the house was poured. The survey does not settle the argument by itself. It puts the argument on paper so the next step — watch it, drain it, investigate it, or repair it — is about the house in front of you and not about a guess from the doorway.

If you need a sentence that says "do this," that sentence needs a stamp. The survey is the picture. It is not the order. SandiaGEO does this work, and can also provide the stamped report when one is warranted — but the data comes first. Get the picture, then decide what it's actually asking for.
