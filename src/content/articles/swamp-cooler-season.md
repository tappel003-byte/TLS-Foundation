Every year around the same time, the calls start sounding alike. A door that's been fine for a decade suddenly won't latch. A window sticks halfway. Someone runs a level across the living room floor for the first time in their life because a neighbor mentioned foundation problems and now they can't stop thinking about it.

Most of the time, the house isn't doing anything new. The season is.

A swamp cooler does not make condensate. Refrigerated air conditioners do that. An evaporative cooler runs water over pads and bleeds some of it off so minerals don't cake the unit. That bleed-off, plus overflow if the float sticks or the reservoir overfills, has to go somewhere. A lot of New Mexico units send it off the roof, down a wall, into a scupper, or onto the dirt next to the stem wall. End of season, someone pulls the drain and dumps the tank. None of that is a flood. It is a slow, steady wet spot in the same few square feet of ground, all summer.

Expansive clay does not need a flood. A few months of water in the same place next to the foundation is enough for that clay to swell.

The tell is usually the calendar, not the crack. A door that sticks in July and swings free again by October is describing a seasonal moisture cycle, not a structural failure. Settlement from a genuine bearing problem does not reverse itself when the cooler gets shut off and the ground dries out. Heave from a wet-dry cycle right next to the house often does, at least partly — which is why it gets read as nothing, or as a disaster, depending on which way someone's fears run.

None of this means it's fine to ignore. A door that binds every summer and eases every fall is telling you something real is happening to the soil under one part of the house, on a schedule. Left alone long enough, a cycle like that can start leaving marks that don't fully reverse — a crack that closes most of the way but not all the way, a door that takes a little more effort each year than it did the year before.

The pattern points somewhere specific. Check where the cooler water is landing. Check the grade. See if summer water is sitting against the foundation before assuming the worst about the house itself. Monsoon and irrigation can be doing the same job in the same months. The cooler is often the first place worth looking, not the only one.
