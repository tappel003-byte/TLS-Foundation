Every year around the same time, the calls start sounding alike. A door that's been fine for a decade suddenly won't latch. A window sticks halfway. Someone runs a level across the living room floor for the first time in their life because a neighbor mentioned foundation problems and now they can't stop thinking about it.

Most of the time, the house isn't doing anything new. The season is.

A swamp cooler doesn't produce condensate the way an AC unit does — it's evaporating water into the air, not pulling it back out. But it still has a bleed-off line that flushes out mineral-heavy water so scale doesn't build up in the reservoir, and a float valve that's supposed to stop it from overflowing. Either one can put water somewhere it shouldn't go: a bleed-off line that terminates six inches from the stem wall, a stuck float valve that's been quietly overflowing onto the roof and running down a wall for years, a leak nobody noticed until the drywall corner started cracking in August. Any of it puts water where the soil right next to the foundation isn't built to handle it. Expansive clay swells when it picks up moisture. It doesn't need a flood to do it. A few months of a slow, steady drip in the same six square feet of dirt is enough.

The tell is usually the calendar, not the crack. A door that sticks in July and swings free again by October is describing a seasonal moisture cycle, not a structural failure. Settlement from a genuine bearing problem doesn't reverse itself when it gets cold. Heave from a wet-dry cycle right next to the house often does, at least partially, which is exactly why it gets mistaken for something less serious than it is, or occasionally something more serious than it is, depending on which direction someone's fears run.

None of this means it's fine to ignore. A door that binds every summer and eases every fall is telling you something real is happening to the soil under one part of the house, on a schedule. Left alone long enough, a cycle like that can start leaving marks that don't fully reverse — a crack that closes most of the way but not all the way, a door that needs a little more effort to latch each year than it did the year before. The pattern matters because it points somewhere specific: check the cooler line, check the grade, see if water is landing where it shouldn't before assuming the worst about the foundation itself.

It's one of the more New Mexico problems there is. Most of the country doesn't run evaporative cooling, and most foundation content written for a national audience has no reason to mention it. Here, it's often the first thing worth checking.
