Some New Mexico soils were laid down dry and have stayed dry since. They hold a house until the first real wetting — a monsoon that finally ponds, a lawn, a leak, a cooler line that got aimed at the wrong dirt. Then the structure of the soil collapses. It doesn't swell back. Settlement from that is not a seasonal door. It is a drop that stays dropped.

You see it on older places when someone finally puts water where there wasn't any, and on newer places built on alluvial fans that looked like good dirt in a dry year. The house was fine. Then it wasn't, and it doesn't recover in October.

Piers can make sense here after the water is controlled, because the soil that collapsed is not going to un-collapse. Piers before the water is controlled just wait for the next wet spot to fail next to the ones you already pinned. Drainage and the leak come first even when the mechanism is collapse instead of heave. The difference is what you expect the ground to do after you stop the water. Heave may give some of it back. This won't.

The tell is the history: dry for decades, then a change, then a one-way move. If that history is on the table, don't read the crack as a summer cycle.
