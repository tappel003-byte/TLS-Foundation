The crack ran diagonally from a window corner, the way they usually do. What made it worth a second look wasn't the crack itself — it was the fact that the homeowner had been watching it for two years and could describe exactly how it behaved. Wider in July and August. Narrower by November. Almost closed by January.

That's not a small detail. It's close to the whole diagnosis.

Settlement is soil giving way under a foundation, and it doesn't reverse itself with the seasons. Once ground compresses or collapses, it stays compressed. Heave is the opposite mechanism entirely — expansive clay swelling as it picks up moisture, then shrinking back as it dries out — and it can produce a crack that behaves exactly like the one in this house: opening in the hottest, wettest stretch of the year, closing back down in the winter. Two different soils doing two different things, and from the floor alone, they can look identical.

The proposal this house had already received before we saw it called for helical piers along the affected wall — a settlement solution, priced and ready to sign. Nobody had asked what the crack did in January. Piers driven to depth do one job well: they transfer load to soil that isn't moving. They do nothing to stop soil that's still swelling and shrinking on its own schedule, and in the worst case, installing them without addressing the moisture cycle just gives the heave something rigid to fight against.

The actual first move here wasn't piers at all. It was figuring out what was driving moisture into the clay at that specific corner of the house — a downspout, a grade that sloped the wrong way, an irrigation line running closer to the foundation than anyone had noticed — and fixing that first. Underpinning may still end up part of the answer once the water source is under control and a real elevation survey confirms what's actually moving and by how much. It just isn't the first answer, and it was never going to be, once the seasonal pattern was on the table.

This is the version of misdiagnosis that doesn't announce itself. Nobody proposed the wrong pier out of carelessness. They proposed the pier that fits the symptom that's easiest to see, without asking the one question — what does this do in winter — that would have told a different story.
