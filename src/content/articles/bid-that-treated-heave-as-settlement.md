The crack ran diagonally from a window corner, the way they usually do. What made it worth a second look wasn't the crack itself — it was that the homeowner had been watching it for two years and could describe exactly how it behaved. Wider in July and August. Narrower by November. Almost closed by January.

That's not a small detail. It's close to the whole diagnosis.

Settlement is soil giving way under a foundation. Once ground compresses or collapses, it stays that way. It does not walk back in the winter. Heave is the opposite mechanism — expansive clay swelling as it picks up moisture, then shrinking as it dries — and it can produce a crack that behaves like this one: opening in the monsoon and the irrigation season, closing back down when the ground dries. Two different soils doing two different things. From the floor alone, they can look identical.

The proposal already on the table called for helical piers along the affected wall — a settlement solution, priced and ready to sign. Nobody had asked what the crack did in January. Piers driven to depth do one job well: they transfer load to soil that isn't moving. They do nothing to stop soil that is still swelling and shrinking on its own schedule. In the worst case, installing them without touching the moisture cycle just gives the heave something rigid to push against.

The first move here wasn't piers. It was finding what was driving moisture into the clay at that corner — a downspout, a grade that sloped the wrong way, an irrigation line closer to the foundation than anyone had noticed — and dealing with that first. Underpinning may still be part of the answer once the water is under control and an elevation survey shows what is actually moving, and by how much. It was never going to be the first answer once the seasonal pattern was on the table.

This is the version of misdiagnosis that doesn't announce itself. Nobody proposed the wrong pier out of carelessness. They proposed the pier that fits the symptom that's easiest to see, without asking the one question — what does this do in winter — that would have told a different story.
