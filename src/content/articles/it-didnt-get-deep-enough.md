The shafts were in. The invoice said they were in. The house was still moving.

A pier is only doing its job if the plates or the point are in soil that can hold the load. Depth on a proposal is a guess until the ground answers. Fill, a soft layer under a hard cap, a clay that looks firm at eight feet and isn't at twelve — the number they planned in the office is not automatically the number that is in the ground.

Short is the usual failure. The crew hit resistance, called it bearing, and cut the shaft. Or the bid was priced to a depth that made the job cheap, and the bad soil went deeper than the price. Torque can lie if you read a crust as capacity. A tape measure on the last section only tells you how much steel you buried, not what that steel is sitting in.

You find it after the fact the same way you find it during the work: another look at the soil, elevations that keep changing on the "repaired" line, a shaft you can still move, a depth that doesn't match the fill you know is there. The fix is not another row of the same pier next to the short ones. It is getting past the layer they stopped in, or saying this tool was never going to reach and changing tools.

Deep enough is not a brand. It is a depth. If the house is still going, start with whether they ever got there.
