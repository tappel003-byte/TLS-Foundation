Valley floors in this state are not a static water line. The North Valley especially — the water table comes up and goes down with the river, the ditches, and the year. A house that was dry at the footing in April can have moisture at that same elevation in August without anyone spilling a glass.

That is not a cooler and it is not a neighbor's lawn. It is the ground itself changing what "dry" means under the slab. Heave and settlement can both show up depending on the soil the water is moving through. The tell is a whole side of the house, or the whole floor, shifting with the season instead of one drip line.

Piers do not lower the water table. If the movement tracks the valley's wet year, the first conversation is drainage, grading, and whether this lot is even a pier problem. Sometimes it is, once you know the high water and the bearing layer are not the same elevation. Sometimes the honest answer is that the site is doing what this valley does and steel will not talk it out of it.

Look at the year. Look at the river. Look at whether last year's dry winter matches last year's tight doors. If it does, you are not looking at a random crack. You are looking at a water table.
